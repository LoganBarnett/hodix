This document records where hodix is headed.  Nothing here is built yet.

* Federation

The first goal after the facts baseline is to express a hodocule.

Any hodon can federate with another.  The owners of both hodons exchange a
handshake.  Each owner states which permissions and resources the other hodon is
granted.  Either party can end the grants or the connection itself at any time.

Hodon names are unique within a hodocule and nowhere wider.  A bridge resolves a
peer's names to one peer, so the federation facts reject a peer whose name
collides with home or with another peer.

** Federated and visited hodons

A hodon relates to another hodon in one of two ways.

A federated hodon is a member of the same hodocule.  The relationship is between
two hodons and both owners agree to it.  It lives in the facts of both.  Home
runs a WireGuard link to the peer, a bridge for the peer's names, and DNS that
sends the peer's names to that bridge.  Devices gain the peer's names in their
tunnel domain list and nothing else.  Only a device that roams onto the peer
trusts its root, and then only for the peer's subtree.

A visited hodon is a venue.  The relationship is between one device and the
venue, and home is not a party to it.  Nothing about the venue enters home's
facts or home's network.  The device installs a constrained anchor for the
venue's root and resolves the venue's names locally while standing there.  What
a venue offers guests, and on which network, is a fact of the venue.  Home
grants nothing and learns nothing.

Both relationships use the same device-side trust.  They differ in whether
home's network takes part.

** Connection

Two federated hodons connect to each other over WireGuard.  The link is peer to
peer across the Internet.  Some esoteric means of direct connection may also
carry it.  Seeing both hodons bring up this link is one of the key proofs we
want.

** Reaching a service on another hodon

A hodocule gains a hodon without any device trusting a new certificate.  A
device that roams onto the new hodon is the exception.  [[*Roaming][Roaming]] bounds what it
trusts.

Each hodon's root certificate covers every name under ~hodon.internal~.  It is
not limited to the hodon's own domain.  Each hodon also runs an nginx instance.
That instance translates TLS with its twin on the other hodon.

Take a client on ~proton~ that wants ~nextcloud.heartwood.hodon.internal~.  The
hodon ~heartwood~ is federated with ~proton~.

1. The client's DNS resolves the name to the nginx instance on ~proton~.
2. That nginx answers under the root certificate of ~proton~.  Every client
   device on ~proton~ already trusts it.
3. It re-encrypts the request under the TLS of ~heartwood~.  The request then
   crosses the WireGuard interface.
4. The nginx instance on ~heartwood~ forwards the request to the Nextcloud
   instance in ~heartwood~.

** Roaming

A device belongs to one hodon.  That hodon is its home.  Away from home, the
device reaches home's names and every federated peer's names through a split
tunnel to home.  The home bridge is the only middle box that ever sees the
device's credentials.  The namespace is shared, so home claims only what home
can answer.  Names of any other hodon resolve on the network the device is
standing on.

The price is a boomerang.  A device standing on a federated hodon reaches that
hodon's own services by way of home.  A later client module may route that
hodon's names locally while the device stands on it.  The DHCP domain is hint
enough.  Trust is TLS's job, so a false claim buys the claimant nothing but a
denial of service it already had.  Home's own names never leave the tunnel.

Trust is asymmetric.  A device trusts its home root for all of ~hodon.internal~.
It trusts any other hodon's root for that hodon's own subtree only.  The device
re-signs the foreign CA key under a name constraint and installs the result as
the anchor.  Validators do not check the signature on an anchor, so no other
party signs anything.  A foreign bridge then cannot present a home name to the
device, so home credentials never transit a foreign bridge.

A hodon with no relationship to home is reachable and untrusted.  Its names
resolve locally and its certificates fail validation until the device visits it.
Every hodon service speaks TLS so that failure is the whole story.
